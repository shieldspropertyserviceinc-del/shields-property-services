❄️ Winter 5 guides

How to prepare your driveway for winter

Winnipeg averages about 126 cm of snow a year. A little prep in October saves you headaches in January:

  • Mark your edges. Put driveway markers or stakes along the edges before the ground freezes. It helps whoever clears your snow avoid chewing up grass or hitting hidden curbs.
  • Trim back branches. Anything hanging over the driveway will get heavy with snow and ice — cut it back now.
  • Move summer gear. Bikes, toys, and planters left on the driveway become obstacles buried under snow.
  • Check your drainage. Make sure downspouts direct water away from the driveway, or you'll get an ice rink by December.
  • Stock up on sand. Even with a clearing service, a bag of sand by the door helps on icy mornings between visits.

Snow clearing triggers explained: 2 cm vs 4 cm vs 6 cm

A "trigger" is the snowfall amount that sends the crew out. Here's how to pick:

  • 6 cm (Essential) — for people who don't mind a bit of snow and mainly want the big dumps handled. Most cost-effective.
  • 4 cm (Complete) — the sweet spot for most Transcona driveways. You will rarely wake up to an uncleared driveway.
  • 2 cm (Premium) — for anyone who needs reliable access every morning, or just hates seeing snow, period.

Winnipeg gets a lot of 2–4 cm dustings that add up. If you are on the fence, the 4 cm trigger covers the vast majority of winter mornings without the premium price.

What is windrow (and why should you care?)

Windrow is the ridge of hard, packed snow the city plow leaves across the end of your driveway. It is the heaviest, iciest snow you will deal with all winter — and it shows up after you have already cleared the rest.

Ice dam prevention on your roof

Ice dams form when heat escaping into the attic melts roof snow unevenly. Meltwater refreezes at the colder eaves, backs up under shingles, and leaks into your home. Winnipeg's freeze-thaw cycles make this one of the most expensive winter roofing problems.

  • Insulate the attic to R-50. That's roughly 16–20 inches of blown-in insulation, evenly distributed. Seal air leaks around chimneys, vents, and light fixtures first.
  • Ventilate properly. Soffit vents at the eaves plus ridge vents at the peak create airflow that keeps the roof deck cold and melting even.
  • Roof-rake the bottom 4–6 feet after heavy snowfalls. Never chip at ice with tools — you'll damage shingles and gutters.
  • Watch for big icicles. Large icicles along the eaves mean water is refreezing there. Act before a full dam forms.
  • March–April is peak season. Daytime sun melts heavy snow, nights refreeze it. Keep valleys clear.

Clogged gutters make dams worse — another reason to get them cleaned in fall.

Pet-safe ice melt: what actually works

"Pet-friendly" is an unregulated marketing term. Many products with the label still contain chlorides that harm pets. Always read the ingredient list.

  • Truly safe means chloride-free. Look for modified urea or glycol-based products with zero sodium, calcium, magnesium, or potassium chloride.
  • Avoid: rock salt (cuts paws), calcium chloride (chemical burns), magnesium chloride (dangerous for dogs with kidney issues), and anything with ethylene glycol (antifreeze — extremely toxic).
  • Wipe paws after every walk regardless of product. Even "safer" melts dry and crack paw pads.
  • Shovel early so less melt product is needed in the first place.

Fall cleanup checklist for Winnipeg homes

Do this before the first lasting snowfall and your spring self will thank you:

  • Clear all leaves. Matted leaves smother grass over winter and invite mold in spring.
  • Pick up branches and droppings. Anything left on the lawn gets pressed into the turf by snow.
  • Clean out garden beds. Remove dead annuals and cut back perennials.
  • Mark sprinkler heads and hidden edges. Flag anything a blower or shovel could hit.
  • Last mow short. Cut the grass a bit shorter than usual for the final mow — it reduces snow mold.

Why gutter cleaning matters before winter

Clogged gutters are a top contributor to ice dams, fascia rot, and foundation damage — all vastly more expensive than a cleaning.

  • Timing: late October to early November, after leaves fall but before the first lasting freeze.
  • Clogged gutters feed ice dams. Debris traps meltwater at the eaves where it refreezes and forces water under shingles.
  • Clear the downspouts too and make sure they discharge at least 4 feet from the foundation.
  • Weight kills gutters. Debris plus ice and snow load can pull gutters right off the fascia.

Clean gutters now, avoid a $2,000 roof repair in February. Ask about adding gutter cleaning to your fall cleanup.

Protecting young trees and shrubs for winter

Winnipeg's −30°C lows, drying winds, and freeze-thaw cycles are brutal on young plantings. A little fall prep goes a long way.

  • Water deeply before freeze-up. Soak the root zone in late October/early November. Winter drought kills more plants than cold alone.
  • Mulch after the ground freezes (late November): 4 inches of wood chips, kept 6 inches from the trunk. No "mulch volcanoes."
  • Wrap young trees with burlap from base to first branches to prevent sunscald and frost cracking. Remove in early April.
  • Guard against rodents with trunk guards 18–24 inches above expected snow depth.
  • Don't pile snow on plantings. It compacts, delays thaw, and can snap branches.

Fall cleanup is the natural time to install wraps, guards, and mulch — just ask.

Winterizing your lawn equipment

Stale fuel is the #1 cause of spring no-starts. An hour of fall maintenance saves a repair bill in April.

  • Fuel: stabilize or drain. Either fill ¾ with fresh gas plus stabilizer and run 5–10 minutes, or run the tank completely dry. Never store half-full and untreated.
  • Change the oil so fresh oil protects the engine all winter.
  • Clean the deck and filter. Scrape caked grass (disconnect the spark plug first), brush or replace the air filter.
  • Sharpen the blade now so the mower is truly ready in spring.
  • Batteries indoors on a smart maintainer, not bare concrete.
  • Don't forget the snowblower — fresh oil, check shear pins and belts before the first snowfall.

When to aerate your lawn in Manitoba

The best time to aerate in Winnipeg is early fall (September) or spring (May) — when the grass is actively growing but the heat is not stressing it.

Signs your lawn needs it: water pools instead of soaking in, the soil feels rock-hard, or thin/bare patches keep coming back despite watering and fertilizing. Clay-heavy Manitoba soil compacts easily, so most lawns benefit from aeration every 1–2 years.

Snow mold: what it is and how to beat it

Those circular straw-coloured patches that appear when snow melts? That's snow mold — a fungal disease. Gray mold affects blades only and usually recovers; pink mold can kill crowns and need reseeding.

  • Prevention starts in fall: final mow at 2–3 inches, clear all leaves, stop nitrogen fertilizer 6 weeks before frost, keep thatch under ½ inch.
  • Don't pile snow in one spot — concentrated piles keep soil cold and wet longer, creating ideal mold conditions.
  • Spring treatment: gently rake matted areas on a dry day to improve airflow. Most lawns recover naturally as temperatures pass 10–15°C.
  • Overseed bare patches where pink mold killed the crowns.

Check shaded areas and low spots first — anywhere snow lingered longest.

Spring dethatching for Manitoba lawns

Thatch is the spongy brown layer between grass blades and soil. Over ½ inch, it blocks water, harbors disease, and starves roots.

  • Timing: mid to late April, once the lawn is dry and daytime highs are above 10°C for a week. Never work wet clay — it compacts.
  • Test first: cut a small wedge and measure. Under ½ inch? Skip it this year.
  • How: rent a power rake, set tines to just tickle the soil, two passes at right angles. It looks brutal; that's normal.
  • Clean up thoroughly — leaving pulled thatch defeats the purpose.
  • Follow with aeration and overseeding for the full lawn revival.
Overseeding: timing and best seed for Winnipeg

Overseeding thickens thin lawns and repairs winter damage. Timing determines whether seed becomes grass or bird food.

  • Best window: late August to mid-September. Warm soil, cool air, fall rains, minimal weed competition.
  • Best seed: Kentucky bluegrass blends (3+ varieties for disease resistance). Handles Winnipeg extremes and self-repairs.
  • Rate: 2–5 lbs per 1,000 sq ft. Buy quality seed — cheap bags contain weed seeds.
  • Prep matters more than seed: mow short, rake to expose soil, aerate first. Water lightly 2–3 times daily until germination.

Summer watering schedule for Winnipeg lawns

Deep, infrequent watering builds deep roots. Daily sprinkling builds shallow, drought-prone turf.

  • The 1-inch rule: lawns need ~1 inch per week including rainfall. Water deeply 1–2 times per week, not daily.
  • Water 4–10 AM. Less evaporation, foliage dries before nightfall. Never midday, never late evening.
  • The footprint test: if footprints stay visible, it's time to water.
  • Mow high: 2.5–3 inches. Taller grass shades roots and cuts water loss.
  • Let it go dormant in heat waves. Brown Kentucky bluegrass in extreme heat is surviving, not dying. It greens up when temperatures drop.

Check the City of Winnipeg's current water conservation rules before setting a schedule — restrictions vary year to year.

Dealing with Winnipeg's heavy clay soil

Winnipeg sits on dense Red River clay — slow to drain, quick to compact, rock-hard when dry. It's behind half of local lawn problems.

  • Compost is the #1 fix. Spread 2–4 inches and work into the top 6–8 inches. This permanently improves structure — repeat annually.
  • Aerate 1–2 times per year. Core aeration physically opens clay. The single highest-ROI service for Winnipeg lawns.
  • Gypsum helps as a supplement (~40 lbs per 1,000 sq ft), not a replacement for compost.
  • Don't add small amounts of sand — it makes clay worse. Only helps at 50%+ ratios with lots of organic matter.
  • Never work wet clay. Wait until it's moist but crumbly or you'll create brick-like clods.
