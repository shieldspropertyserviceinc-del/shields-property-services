Winnipeg winter tips: getting ahead of the first big snowLocal know-how for getting ahead of the first big snow — and the deep freeze after it.

Winnipeg winter rewards people who prepare early — and punishes procrastination like nowhere else. The first big snowfall of the season tends to arrive before most households feel ready for it, and once it lands, everything from city plowing schedules to driveway windrows runs on winter's timetable, not yours. Here are the Winnipeg-specific things worth knowing before the first major storm.

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

1. Know your snow zone before a parking ban is called. Every residential street in Winnipeg is assigned a snow zone letter. When the City calls a Residential Parking Ban after a significant snowfall, zones are plowed in 12-hour shifts — 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., or 7 p.m. to 7 a.m. — and parking is prohibited on your street during your zone's scheduled shift. A ticket for violating the ban is $200 ($150 if paid early). Find your zone now, while the weather is calm: use the City's Know Your Zone app or online address lookup, or call 311. Also remember the annual winter route parking ban: no parking between 2 a.m. and 7 a.m. on streets signed as snow routes. That one applies all winter, whether or not a residential ban is in effect.

2. Clear in passes, not in one heroic effort. During a major Winnipeg snowfall, waiting until it is "all over" usually means moving snow that has been driven on, packed down, and partially frozen — heavier, slower work that is harder on people and equipment. If conditions are safe, clearing once partway through a storm and again after it ends is almost always easier. At minimum, avoid driving over fresh snow before clearing; tire tracks compress it into ridges that freeze and linger for weeks.

3. Have a windrow plan — and know whose job it is. The ridge a plow leaves across the end of a driveway is called a windrow, and in Winnipeg the rules depend on where it is:

  • Front street driveways: Windrows over 20 cm (8 inches) high are normally removed by City crews as part of the street plowing operation, in the same shift. In an emergency snow condition that clearing can be delayed — so if you need to get out early, do not count on the windrow being gone.
  • Back lanes: Windrows left by back lane plowing are not removed by the City. They are the homeowner's responsibility, and the City's advice is to move them as soon as possible, before they freeze and become far harder to deal with.

Either way: deal with the windrow promptly. Fresh plow snow is heavy; frozen plow snow is a construction project.

4. Expect drifting, even when it is not snowing. Winnipeg's winter wind is as much a factor as snowfall itself. A driveway cleared in the evening can be drifted back in by morning, especially on open lots, corner properties, and streets with little shelter from the wind. After a windy night, check the driveway, walkway, and back lane entrance before assuming the job is still done — and watch for drifts building against doors, vents, and garage doors.

5. Adjust ice control to the temperature. Standard rock salt loses effectiveness as temperatures drop well below −15°C, so in deep cold it often makes more sense to use sand for traction and save ice melt for milder days, sunny afternoons, and freeze-thaw periods around 0°C, when it can actually do its job. Never use boiling water on an icy driveway — it can crack concrete and refreezes into a smoother, more dangerous sheet of ice. And store melt and sand somewhere dry and reachable now, not behind summer equipment in a frozen shed.

6. Mark, clear, and keep visible.

  • Put reflective markers along driveway edges and near walkway corners before the ground freezes.
  • Know where your furnace and dryer vents and intakes are, and keep them clear of snow and drifts all winter.
  • Keep your house number visible from the street; it matters for deliveries and emergencies alike.
  • Pile cleared snow away from the foundation, and never onto the street or a neighbour's property.

7. Decide on help before the first storm, not during it. The busiest day of the year for any Winnipeg snow service is the day of the first big snowfall. Seasonal plans arranged in advance get built into routes; requests made mid-storm join a queue. Shields Property Services' snow season runs November through March, with visits within 15 hours of snowfall ending and plans from $150/month, starting with a free estimate — online or in-person — in Transcona. Whether you hire help or handle it yourself, the decision is easier — and the options are better — when it is made in October instead of during a storm in November.

Quick tips — the one-minute list. A few more that each take under a minute:

  • Snap a photo of your driveway and walkway now, before snow covers the edges, steps, and sprinkler heads — you will be glad to have the reference in February.
  • Disconnect and drain garden hoses before the hard freeze. A hose left attached can freeze back into the tap and burst a pipe inside the wall.
  • Brush the whole car, not just the windshield — roof, hood, lights, and plates. Roof snow blows off into the windshields of everyone behind you.
  • Keep snow piles low at the end of the driveway so you can see traffic, cyclists, and kids when backing out.
  • Apply ice melt after clearing, not before. Melt spread before you shovel just gets shovelled away; melt spread after clearing works on what's left and helps before the evening refreeze.
  • Keep a second, small shovel at the back door for steps and the deck, so you are not hauling the big shovel through the house.
  • In a cold snap, keep the fuel tank at least half full and check tire pressure — pressure drops as the temperature falls.
  • Push slush off the driveway after a mild day, before it refreezes overnight into ruts and ridges that last for weeks.

The short version: Know your snow zone. Respect the parking bans. Clear in passes. Move windrows early, especially in the back lane. Use sand in deep cold and save melt for near 0°C. Winnipeg winter is entirely manageable. It just does not negotiate.

How to shovel snow safelyTechnique, pacing, and the warning signs that mean stop — shovel without injuring yourself.

Shovelling looks simple, and that's the problem — it's hard exercise done in cold air, usually in a hurry before work. Most injuries aren't from one big lift, they're from rushing, twisting, and trying to move snow that's already packed down. Technique beats strength every time:

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

  • Push, don't lift. Walk the snow to the edge of the driveway instead of throwing it. When you do have to lift, take small loads, bend your knees, keep the load close to your body, and never twist at the waist — turn your feet instead.
  • Shovel early, and more than once. Fresh snow is light. The same snow after cars and feet have packed it — or after a freeze overnight — can weigh several times as much. During a big storm, a quick pass mid-storm is easier than one huge clear at the end.
  • Pick the right shovel. A lightweight pusher for most snow, a smaller scoop for lifting, and a handle long enough that you're not hunched over. A shovel that's too big just tempts you to lift too much at once.
  • Pace yourself. Take breaks before you're exhausted, and drink water — it's easy to get dehydrated in cold air without feeling thirsty. Switch sides regularly so one shoulder and arm don't do all the work.
  • Dress for traction, not just warmth. Layers you can open up as you warm up, and boots with real tread. Most shovelling falls happen on the packed, slippery strip you haven't cleared yet.
  • Know the stop signs. Chest pain or tightness, unusual shortness of breath, dizziness, or nausea are not "push through it" signals — stop, go inside, and get medical help if they don't pass quickly. If you have a heart condition or haven't been active in a while, talk to your doctor about whether shovelling is safe for you at all.
  • Clear a path to your vents and meter while you're out. Keep furnace and water-heater vents, your gas meter, and your dryer vent dug out by hand — see our carbon monoxide guide for why this one matters.

There is no prize for finishing fastest. A cleared driveway in twenty steady minutes beats a cleared driveway and a bad back — or worse — in ten.

The short version: Push, don't lift — small loads, knees bent, never twist. Shovel early and more than once; fresh snow weighs a fraction of packed snow. Pace yourself, dress for traction, and treat chest pain, tightness, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath as stop-now signs, not push-through signs.

Ice dam prevention on your roofWhy ice dams form at the roof edge, the damage they cause, and how to prevent them.

Ice dams form when heat escaping into the attic melts roof snow unevenly. Meltwater refreezes at the colder eaves, backs up under shingles, and leaks into your home. Winnipeg's freeze-thaw cycles make this one of the most expensive winter roofing problems.

  • Insulate the attic to R-50. That's roughly 16–20 inches of blown-in insulation, evenly distributed. Seal air leaks around chimneys, vents, and light fixtures first.
  • Ventilate properly. Soffit vents at the eaves plus ridge vents at the peak create airflow that keeps the roof deck cold and melting even.
  • Roof-rake the bottom 4–6 feet after heavy snowfalls. Never chip at ice with tools — you'll damage shingles and gutters.
  • Watch for big icicles. Large icicles along the eaves mean water is refreezing there. Act before a full dam forms.
  • March–April is peak season. Daytime sun melts heavy snow, nights refreeze it. Keep valleys clear.

Clogged gutters make dams worse — another reason to get them cleaned in fall.

Roof snow load: how much snow is too much? NewHow much snow a roof can actually hold, the warning signs, and when to act.

Most pitched roofs in Winnipeg handle a normal winter just fine — the city averages about 126 cm of snow a season, and roofs here are built for it. The trouble isn't one snowfall, it's weight building up: wet spring snow, drifted snow piled on one side, and ice on top of snow. Fresh powder is light. The same depth of wet, heavy snow can weigh several times as much.

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

Where the risk actually is. Flat and low-slope roofs, sheds, carports, porches, and older additions are the ones to watch first. So are roofs where wind piles snow into deep drifts on one side, and roofs with a heavy ice dam already sitting at the edge — that ice adds weight right where the roof is already working hardest. A normal pitched roof with snow spread evenly across it is a very different situation from a flat garage roof with a drift on one corner.

Signs your roof is overloaded. Look inside, not just outside. New cracks in the ceiling or upper walls, a ceiling that looks like it's sagging, doors or windows on the top floor that suddenly stick, and creaking or groaning sounds from the roof are all warning signs. Outside, look for a visible sag in the roofline, or leaks appearing in the middle of winter with no obvious ice dam. One of these on its own might be something else — two or more together means take it seriously.

What to do about it. Clear snow from the ground with a roof rake, in small sections, working across the roof rather than pulling a huge slab down at once. Clear after heavy or wet snowfalls, not just at the end of the season, and don't let drifts sit for weeks. Clear the roof edge first — that's where ice dams form, and our ice dam prevention guide has the full method. Never climb on a snowy roof, never use a ladder to get up there in winter, and never chip at ice with a shovel or axe — you'll damage the shingles and the roof underneath, and a slip on an icy roof is the real danger.

When to call a professional. If you see sagging, cracking, or sticking doors, don't try to clear it yourself to fix the problem — get a roofer or structural professional to look at it first. Same for a flat roof you can't safely reach from the ground, a two-storey roof, or a roof with a thick ice dam already formed. Clearing snow helps prevent overload, it doesn't repair a roof that's already stressed.

The short version: Normal pitched roofs handle a Winnipeg winter. Watch flat roofs, sheds, carports, and drifted corners. New cracks, sagging, sticking doors, or creaking mean get a professional in. Clear from the ground with a roof rake after heavy or wet snow — never climb up there.

Roof-edge clearing: when your roof edge needs it, and how to do it safely NewClearing the bottom edge of the roof safely from the ground — when it helps and how it is done.

Ice dams start at the roof edge. Heat escaping into the attic melts snow higher up the roof, the water runs down, and it refreezes at the colder eaves — backing up under the shingles and leaking inside. Clearing the bottom few feet of the roof edge after a big snowfall removes the snow that feeds that cycle. It's prevention, not ice dam removal — once a dam has formed and water is leaking in, that's a different job.

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

When to clear it. After snowfalls of 15 cm or more, after wet heavy snow, and in March and April when daytime sun melts heavy snow and nights refreeze it — that's peak ice dam season. Also clear it if you're seeing big icicles along the eaves, which mean water is refreezing there. You don't need to clear the edge after every light dusting, and you don't need to clear the whole roof — the bottom 3 to 4 feet is the zone that matters for ice dams. Our ice dam prevention guide has the full picture on insulation and ventilation, which is what stops dams long-term.

How to do it from the ground. Use a telescoping roof rake — a long-handled rake with a wide blade, made for this. Stand on the ground, well back from the eaves, and pull snow down in small sections, working across the edge a bit at a time. Let the snow fall clear of you, and keep your footing — the ground under the eaves is icy, and chunks of snow and ice will be coming down. Work in daylight, and stop if the rake catches on a shingle or gutter. Never pull a huge slab down in one go; it comes down fast and heavy.

What not to do. Never climb on the roof, and never use a ladder to reach the edge in winter — that's how most roof injuries happen, and it's not worth it for a job a rake does from the ground. Never use a metal shovel, axe, or hammer to chip at ice — you'll damage shingles and gutters, and the ice will just come back. Don't stand directly under the eaves while you're working, don't work under power lines, and don't clear a steep or two-storey roof from a ladder because the rake won't reach — that's a professional job. And don't pile the cleared snow against the house; throw it out onto the lawn.

When to stop and call someone. If there's already a thick ice dam and water leaking inside, if the roof is steep or two storeys and you can't reach the edge safely from the ground, or if the rake keeps catching and you're tempted to climb up to "just finish it" — stop. Full-roof clearing and ice dam removal need different equipment and, for height work, different insurance. Clearing the edge is the prevention slice; it isn't the whole roof.

Pet-safe ice melt: what actually worksWhich ice melts are safer around dogs and cats, and how to use any melt with less risk.

"Pet-friendly" is an unregulated marketing term. Many products with the label still contain chlorides that harm pets. Always read the ingredient list.

  • Truly safe means chloride-free. Look for modified urea or glycol-based products with zero sodium, calcium, magnesium, or potassium chloride.
  • Avoid: rock salt (cuts paws), calcium chloride (chemical burns), magnesium chloride (dangerous for dogs with kidney issues), and anything with ethylene glycol (antifreeze — extremely toxic).
  • Wipe paws after every walk regardless of product. Even "safer" melts dry and crack paw pads.
  • Shovel early so less melt product is needed in the first place.

How to clear an icy driveway yourselfChop, melt, and traction: a step-by-step for clearing packed ice off your driveway yourself.

Driveway ice is rarely new snow — it's snow that got packed down by tires and feet, melted a little in the sun or a mild afternoon, then refroze into a sheet. That's why it doesn't shovel off, and why the fix is timing and technique, not force:

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

  • Prevention beats scraping. Clear snow completely, and clear it early — before anyone drives or walks on it. Packed snow is tomorrow's ice. And check your downspouts: a downspout draining across the driveway will rebuild the same ice patch all winter.
  • Pick your moment. Ice lets go easiest on a sunny afternoon or when temperatures climb toward 0°C. The bond to the pavement weakens first — the same sheet that's immovable at −20°C in the morning often lifts in pieces a few hours later.
  • Scrape, don't chop. Use a long-handled steel ice scraper at a low angle and work from an edge or a crack, getting the blade under the sheet and lifting it in sections. Chopping straight down chips concrete, cracks paving stones, and still leaves most of the ice behind.
  • Protect your surface. Go gently on paving stones, stamped or sealed concrete, and older cracked pavement — keep the scraper flat, never pry against a stone's edge. And never scrape a vehicle: a scraper that is fine on pavement will scratch paint and can crack a cold windshield.
  • Give melt product time to work. On thick ice, spread ice melt first and wait — it loosens the bond so the scraper can get underneath. Rock salt stops working well in deep cold; calcium chloride keeps working colder (but read our pet-safe ice melt guide first if you have dogs or cats — some products aren't safe for them).
  • Sand is for grip, not melting. Once the worst ice is off — or if it's too cold or too thick to remove today — a thin scatter of sand gives tires and boots traction right away. It won't clear the ice, and you'll sweep up what's left in spring.
  • Never use boiling water. It can crack cold concrete, and what doesn't crack the driveway usually just refreezes into a smoother, slipperier sheet.
  • Work like it's slippery — because it is. Boots with tread, short steps, and scrape facing uphill of the patch where you can. Most driveway falls happen while someone is swinging a tool on the very ice they're trying to remove.

Whatever you can't lift today, sand it, mark it mentally, and come back on the next mild afternoon. Ice built up over weeks rarely comes off in one session — and a driveway doesn't have to be perfect to be safe.

The short version: Clear snow early, before it's packed into ice. Work on a mild, sunny afternoon, scrape at a low angle from an edge — don't chop. Melt product first on thick ice, sand for grip, and never use boiling water.

Every Winnipeg driver will use this eventually. Safety first, then technique:

⚠️ Carbon monoxide & your furnace ventsBlocked furnace and dryer vents can push carbon monoxide indoors — where to check after a storm.

High-efficiency furnaces and water heaters don't vent up a chimney — they breathe through plastic pipes low on the side of your house, exactly where snowdrifts pile up. If those pipes get buried, the furnace can shut down on the coldest night of the year. Worse, carbon monoxide can be pushed back into your home instead of out of it.

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

Carbon monoxide is colourless and odourless — you can't see it, smell it, or taste it. The symptoms feel like the flu without the fever: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion. By the time it clicks, your thinking may already be impaired. That's why prevention and alarms matter more than trying to detect it yourself.

After every storm, do a two-minute vent walk:

  • Find your pipes. Look for two plastic pipes — intake and exhaust — coming through the wall near your furnace, usually just above the foundation, often on the side of the house where drifts build deepest.
  • Clear a metre of space. Dig snow and ice away by hand until there's at least a metre (about 3 feet) of clear space around the pipes. Cold plastic is brittle — never chop at the pipes with a shovel or scraper.
  • Check the other vents too. Dryer vents, fresh-air intakes, and the vent on your gas meter/regulator all need to stay clear. And never pile shovelled snow against the gas meter.
  • Repeat after drifting. Wind can rebuild a drift against the house overnight, even when no new snow has fallen.

Garages are a trap. Never idle a vehicle, run a generator, or run a snow blower inside a garage — even with the door open. Warm the car up outside in the driveway, not in the garage doorway.

CO alarms are non-negotiable. Keep one on every floor and outside sleeping areas. Test them monthly, and replace them by the manufacturer's expiry date — the sensor wears out even if the test beep still sounds. If an alarm goes off, get everyone — including pets — outside first, then call 911 from outside. Don't stop to open windows or hunt for the source.

When we clear your driveway and walkway after a storm, that's one stretch of your home dug out — but the vent check takes two minutes, and it's worth doing yourself after every big drift.

The short version: After every storm, find the two plastic pipes low on your wall and clear a metre around them, by hand. Check dryer and meter vents too, never run a vehicle or blower in a garage, and keep a CO alarm on every floor. If one goes off: everyone outside first, then 911 from outside.

Snow blower basics: run it safely and make it lastFuel, shear pins, safe starting, and clearing technique for your first winters with a blower.

A snow blower is wonderful right up until it is not. Almost every blower problem — the clog, the dead pull cord, the auger that spins but throws nothing — comes from a small set of avoidable mistakes. Here is how to run one safely and keep it working for years.

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

Before the first snow: the 20-minute prep. Drain or replace last season's fuel — old gasoline is the number one reason blowers refuse to start. If the fuel is fresh, add stabilizer. Check the oil level. Look at the shear pins (small bolts through the auger shaft — more on these below) and put two spares in your pocket or the machine's storage box; you will thank yourself in February. Check that the skid shoes and scraper bar are not worn through, and know where the clog-clearing tool is — most machines ship with one clipped to the handle.

Never clear a clog with your hands. This is the big one. Most serious snow blower injuries are hands in the discharge chute. The auger can keep turning or spring back after the engine stops. The procedure is always the same: turn the engine OFF, wait until the auger has fully stopped, then clear the clog with the clearing tool or a stick. No exceptions, even when you are in a hurry and the neighbours are watching.

Keep people and pets clear of the discharge. A blower can throw ice chunks and hidden debris a long way. Shut down or redirect if anyone walks into the discharge zone. And never run the machine inside a garage or shed, even with the door open — the exhaust will fill the space with carbon monoxide faster than you think.

Throw snow smart. Point the chute downwind so it does not blow back into your face, and throw snow onto your own property — onto the lawn, never into the street. For deep snow, take half-width passes instead of forcing the full width; the machine works faster and clogs less. Clear in the same pattern each time: start upwind, overlap each pass slightly, and leave the windrow at the end of the driveway for last, while it is still fresh and has not frozen into a wall.

Know your shear pins. Shear pins are small, deliberately weak bolts that connect the auger to its shaft. When the auger hits something hard — a frozen newspaper, a chunk of ice — the pin snaps instead of your gearbox. That is the design working. The symptom of a broken pin: the auger spins (or one side stops) but throws little or no snow. The fix takes two minutes: engine off, knock out the broken pin pieces, slide in a new one. This is why you carry spares. Never replace a shear pin with a regular bolt — the "upgrade" just moves the breakage to the gearbox, which is the expensive part.

End of season: put it away right. Either run the tank dry or fill it with stabilized fuel — do not leave half a tank of untreated gas to go stale all summer. Store the machine covered and dry. When fall comes, fresh fuel and the 20-minute prep, and it starts on the first or second pull.

The short version: Fresh fuel, oil, and spare shear pins before winter. Hands never go in the chute — engine off, wait, use the tool. Throw with the wind onto your lawn. A blower treated this way starts every storm and dies of old age, not neglect.

Snowblower care & storage: keep it starting every winter NewEnd-of-season storage and pre-season checks that keep a blower starting every winter.

Most snowblowers don't die in November. They die in April — from how they were put away. Stale fuel gums up the carburetor, moisture rusts the auger, and a belt left slack all summer cracks. A little care after each storm, and one proper put-away in spring, is what makes a blower start on the first or second pull year after year.

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

After every storm: the 5-minute put-away. Let the auger and impeller run for a few seconds after the last pass, with the chute pointed somewhere safe, to throw out the last wet snow. Then turn the engine off and wait until everything has fully stopped. Brush snow off the chute, housing, and handles, and clear any packed snow from around the auger — with the clearing tool or a stick, never your hands, and with the spark plug wire disconnected if you're reaching in close. Check the shear pins while you're there: if one side of the auger isn't turning, a pin has snapped. Leave the machine to dry, not dripping wet in a corner — a damp blower rusts from the inside out.

Between storms: store it dry and ready. Keep it in a garage or shed, covered if you can, on level ground. Keep the fuel tank fairly full between storms — a half-empty tank breathes moist air in and out as temperatures swing, and that moisture ends up in the fuel. Leave the fuel shutoff valve open during the season, so it's ready to start, and know where it is — a closed valve is the classic "it won't start" that isn't a fault at all. If yours has electric start, keep the battery charged through winter; a flat battery on a -30 morning is a pull-start morning.

Mid-winter check: once a month, ten minutes. Check the oil level on level ground, with the engine cold — top up with the oil your manual specifies, and never run it low. Look at the belts for cracks, fraying, or slack, and at the skid shoes and scraper bar for wear; worn shoes let the auger scrape concrete. Grease any grease fittings your machine has, and check the tire pressure if it's a wheeled model. Spray a little silicone lubricant on the inside of the chute and auger housing — wet snow slides off instead of packing and clogging. If the plug is easy to reach, pull it and look: tan and dry is healthy, black and sooty means it's running rich or the plug is worn, wet with fuel means it's flooded or there's no spark.

End of season: the proper summer storage. This is the step that decides next November. Clean the whole machine first — brush off salt and grit, wash the housing if it's filthy, and let it dry completely. Change the oil while the engine is still warm from its last run, so fresh oil protects it all summer. Fit a fresh spark plug, and check the belts and shear pins one last time, so you're replacing parts in April, not in a blizzard.

Then deal with the fuel — pick one of two good options, and do it properly. Either fill the tank with fresh fuel plus stabilizer, run the engine for five minutes so treated fuel reaches the carburetor, then close the shutoff valve and let it run until it dies. Or drain the tank completely, then open the carburetor bowl drain screw and drain the bowl too, so it's bone dry. What kills blowers is the third option: half a tank of untreated fuel left to go stale in the carburetor all summer. Old fuel goes to a 4R Winnipeg Depot, never down the drain.

Summer storage: where and how. Store it covered, dry, and level, away from fertilizer, salt, and chemicals — their fumes corrode metal. Keep it out of direct sun if it's outside under a cover. Mice love a quiet blower: stuff a rag in the intake and exhaust openings, and check them before you start it in fall. If it has a battery, take it indoors and keep it on a smart maintainer.

Fall wake-up: before the first snow. Don't wait for a forecast. In October, put in fresh fuel — not last March's gas — check the oil, check the plug, check the belts and shear pins, and start it. Let it run for a few minutes, engage the auger briefly to check it throws, and fix anything now, while shops still have parts and time. Our snow blower basics guide has the full 20-minute pre-season prep if you want the checklist version.

Won't start after storage? Work in this order. Fresh fuel first — drain the old and refill. Check the shutoff valve is open, the choke and primer are set right for a cold engine, and the tank cap vent isn't blocked with ice or dirt. Pull the plug: dry means fuel isn't reaching the engine, wet with fuel but no fire means check the spark. Surging up and down usually means a partially clogged jet from stale fuel — cleaning the jet comes before adjusting any screws. If it still won't run right after fresh fuel and a clean jet, that's a carburetor rebuild or a coil fault, and it's shop time, not a bigger hammer.

The short version: After each storm, clear it, dry it, check the pins. Between storms, keep it dry with a full tank. Once a month, check oil, belts, shoes, and the plug. In spring, clean it, change the oil, and either stabilize the fuel properly or drain it completely dry. In fall, fresh fuel and a test run before the snow. A blower treated this way starts every storm and dies of old age, not neglect.

Fall cleanup guide for Winnipeg homesThe full Winnipeg fall cleanup, in order — against the hard deadline of lasting snow.

A Winnipeg fall cleanup runs against a hard deadline: whatever is left on the yard when the lasting snow arrives stays there until April — pressed into the turf, soaking the beds, and feeding mold all winter. The good news is that a thorough cleanup is mostly a weekend of steady work. Here is the full job, in the order that works best in Manitoba's short fall.

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

1. Time it in two passes. Trees in Winnipeg do not drop their leaves all at once, so one giant cleanup rarely works. Do a first pass in mid-to-late October to clear the early drop, then a final pass after most leaves are down but before the lasting snow and deep freeze — usually late October into early November. If snow beats you to the last of the leaves, prioritize the lawn and walkways and finish beds in spring; matted leaves on grass do the real damage.

2. Get the leaves off the lawn. A thick layer of leaves smothers grass, traps moisture, and sets up snow mold over winter. Light, dry coverage can be mulch-mowed in place — see our leaf mulching vs bagging guide for when that works. Heavy, wet, or matted leaves should be raked up and removed. Clear them out of window wells, from under decks, along fence lines, and anywhere they pile against the foundation. For disposal, the City collects yard waste every two weeks in fall: use paper yard waste bags, a reusable container without a lid, or cardboard boxes — never plastic bags — keep containers under 22 kg, and bundle branches no longer than one metre and no thicker than 10 cm. Curbside collection ends in late November, with the last date depending on your collection area, and the 4R Winnipeg Depots take larger loads. Never rake leaves onto the street: they plug drains and can draw a fine.

3. Finish the lawn properly. Keep mowing as long as the grass keeps growing, and make the final cut a little shorter than usual — around 6 cm — without scalping it. A shorter final cut resists matting and snow mold; scalping stresses the roots going into winter. Pick up branches, fruit and tree droppings, toys, and anything else that would sit on the turf under snow for five months. A fall fertilizer application feeds root growth before freeze-up and greens the lawn up faster in spring.

4. Clean out the garden beds. Pull dead annuals and remove foliage from perennials that are prone to disease, such as peonies and iris, so problems do not overwinter in the bed. Cut back most spent perennials, but leave ornamental grasses and sturdy seed heads standing — they add winter structure, shelter beneficial insects, and catch insulating snow. Give the beds one last weeding, since every weed removed now is dozens you will not fight in spring. Hold off on fresh mulch until the ground has cooled, as covered in our tree and shrub protection guide.

5. Protect trees and shrubs. Young plantings go into winter far better after a deep watering before freeze-up, and wraps, trunk guards, and proper mulch make a real difference in Winnipeg's freeze-thaw cycles and drying winds. Our guide on protecting young trees and shrubs has the full method — fall cleanup is the natural time to do it.

6. Clear gutters and move water away. Once the leaves are down, clean the gutters and downspouts so meltwater drains instead of freezing at the eaves — clogged gutters are a leading cause of the ice dams covered in our ice dam guide. Make sure downspouts discharge well away from the foundation. Then shut off the outdoor water supply, open the outside tap to drain the line, and disconnect, drain, and store garden hoses indoors; a hose left connected can freeze back into the tap and split a pipe inside the wall.

7. Mark, store, and service. Before the ground freezes, stake the driveway edges and flag sprinkler heads, low walls, and anything else a shovel or blower could hit under snow. Store or cover patio furniture, bring cushions in, and empty clay and ceramic pots, which crack when wet soil freezes inside them.   • Waiting for every last leaf. Snow does not wait; a 90% cleanup done in October beats a perfect one planned for after the first blizzard.

  • Pruning shrubs in fall. Most pruning waits for late winter or spring; fall cuts can push tender growth and open wounds to the cold.
  • Leaving the hose connected. It is the cheapest pipe burst in Winnipeg to prevent, and one of the most common.

Watering through the seasons: a Winnipeg calendarA month-by-month watering calendar tuned to Winnipeg’s seasons and clay soil.

Your lawn's water needs change dramatically across Winnipeg's seasons. Here's the year-round playbook:

  • Spring (April–May): hold off. Snowmelt usually provides plenty of moisture. Don't start watering until the soil is dry 2 inches down and daytime highs are consistently above 10°C. Watering too early on saturated clay just pools and compacts.
  • Late spring (May–June): wake it up. As growth kicks in, shift to deep watering 1–2 times per week. This is prime time for new seed — keep seeded areas consistently moist with light daily watering until germination.
  • Summer (July–August): deep and early. 1 inch per week including rain, between 4–10 AM. Mow high (2.5–3 inches) to shade roots. Let bluegrass go dormant in extreme heat rather than fighting it.
  • The footprint test: if footprints stay visible in the grass, it's time to water.
  • Early fall (September): taper down. Cooler nights and fall rains reduce needs. Keep watering new fall seed, but established lawns need less. This is the time to aerate — it helps fall rain soak in instead of running off clay.
  • Late fall (October–November): the deep soak. Before the ground freezes, give lawns, trees, and shrubs one last deep watering. Winter drought — roots unable to draw frozen water while wind strips moisture — kills more plants than cold alone. This is the most overlooked watering of the year.
  • Winter: hands off outdoors. No watering needed, but make sure downspouts direct meltwater away from foundations during thaws.

Dealing with Winnipeg's heavy clay soilHeavy, sticky, and slow to drain: working with the soil most Winnipeg yards actually have.

Winnipeg sits on dense Red River clay — slow to drain, quick to compact, rock-hard when dry. It's behind half of local lawn problems.

  • Compost is the #1 fix. Spread 2–4 inches and work into the top 6–8 inches. This permanently improves structure — repeat annually.
  • Aerate 1–2 times per year. Core aeration physically opens clay. The single highest-ROI service for Winnipeg lawns.
  • Gypsum helps as a supplement (~40 lbs per 1,000 sq ft), not a replacement for compost.
  • Don't add small amounts of sand — it makes clay worse. Only helps at 50%+ ratios with lots of organic matter.
  • Never work wet clay. Wait until it's moist but crumbly or you'll create brick-like clods.

Aeration is our flagship clay-soil service — and the reason we recommend it so often in Winnipeg.

How to relevel a heaved paving stone NewLift, level, and re-sand heaved paving stones — a weekend fix, step by step.

Winnipeg clay heaves — water gets under a paving stone, freezes, and lifts one corner. By spring you've got a stone that rocks, a lip to trip on, and water pooling where it shouldn't. One or two stones is an afternoon job. You don't need to rebuild the whole walkway.

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

When to do it. Wait until the ground has thawed and dried out, usually late spring. A stone that looks heaved in April often settles back on its own once the ground dries — give it a few weeks after thaw before deciding it's broken, the same as with a shifted fence post. Work on a dry day, on dry ground. Never try to relevel on wet clay; you'll just make brick-like clods, as our clay-soil guide explains.

What you need. A pry bar or flat shovel to lift the stone, a rubber mallet, a spirit level and a string line, coarse bedding sand (concrete sand, not fine play sand), a hand tamper, a broom, and polymeric sand for the joints if your walkway uses it. Gloves help — paving stones are heavier than they look.

The method, step by step. Mark the heaved stones first, and check the slope: water should run away from the house, not toward it. Pry the stone up carefully and set it aside. Scrape out the old bedding sand underneath and check the gravel base — if the base is solid, you only need to fix the sand layer. If the base has sunk or washed out, top it up with gravel and tamp it firm. Add bedding sand to about an inch deep, screed it level with a straight board, and check it with the level and string line against the neighbouring stones. Set the stone back in, tap it down gently with the rubber mallet until it's level with its neighbours, and check again — level across the stone, and in line with the walkway. Sweep joint sand into the gaps, tamp lightly, sweep again, and mist it with water if you're using polymeric sand, following the bag's instructions.

Mistakes that make it worse. Don't just pile sand on top of the old bed and drop the stone back — it will heave again next winter. Don't set a stone in concrete to "fix" it; concrete locks the stone in and the next heave cracks it instead. Don't ignore the slope — a perfectly level stone that sends water toward your foundation is worse than a slightly uneven one that drains away. And don't relevel a whole walkway one stone at a time if the base has failed everywhere; that's a rebuild, not a repair.

When it's not a small fix. A whole section that's sunk, stones that are cracked through, a walkway that pools water against the house no matter what you do, or a base that's washed out under several stones — those need the base rebuilt properly. There's no shame in that; walkways on Winnipeg clay have a lifespan, and a proper base is what buys the next ten years.

The short version: Wait for thaw and dry ground. Lift the stone, fix the sand bed underneath — about an inch, screeded level — reset it flush with its neighbours, and keep water draining away from the house. Don't pile sand on top, don't set it in concrete, and if the base has failed everywhere, it's a rebuild.

Spring fertilizer schedule for Manitoba lawnsWhat to apply and when: a Manitoba spring feeding schedule that won’t burn the lawn.

Timing matters more than product. Fertilizing too early is one of the most common Manitoba lawn mistakes:

  • Wait until grass is growing. Don't fertilize until soil hits 10°C and the lawn is greening — typically mid-to-late May in Winnipeg. Cold soil wastes product.
  • The Manitoba schedule: late spring (mid-May–June, high-nitrogen) → early summer (late June, balanced, light rate) → late summer (August, slow-release) → fall winterizer (late Sept–Oct, high-potassium). The fall application matters most.
  • Read the bag: N-P-K. Nitrogen = leaf growth, phosphorus = roots, potassium = stress resistance. Spring wants nitrogen; fall wants potassium.
  • Aerate first on clay. Nutrients can't reach roots through compacted clay — aerate so fertilizer soaks in instead of running off.
  • Apply on dry grass, then water in. Morning is ideal. Never apply extra "for good measure" — overdoing nitrogen burns lawns.

Lawn grubs: how to spot and treat themSoft, spongy turf that lifts like carpet? Spot grub damage and treat it at the right time.

White grubs (beetle larvae) eat grass roots — but the animals digging for them often do worse damage than the grubs themselves.

  • Signs: brown patches that expand, spongy turf, grass that lifts like a carpet. The giveaway: skunks, raccoons, or crows tearing up the lawn.
  • Confirm before treating. Cut a 30×30 cm sod square, fold it back, count. Around 8–10 grubs per square foot warrants action.
  • Timing: young grubs near the surface in August–September are the easiest target. Match treatment to the vulnerable window.
  • Treatment: beneficial nematodes (applied to moist soil in the evening, pet/kid safe) are the eco-friendly standard. Btg products target grubs with low non-target risk.
  • Prevention: mow high (2.5–3 inches), aerate, dethatch. A vigorous lawn outgrows moderate grub feeding.

Healthy, aerated lawns resist grubs naturally — another reason we push aeration so hard in Winnipeg.

Restoring a neglected or overgrown yardA step-by-step plan for bringing a neglected or overgrown yard back, one zone at a time.

Inherited a jungle? A yard that's been ignored for a season or more can come back — but work in stages, not all at once:

  • Phase 1 — Clear: remove trash, debris, and dead material. Cut overgrown grass in stages — never more than one-third of the blade at once. Start high, work down over several mowings.
  • Phase 2 — Assess: once you can see the ground, evaluate. What's worth saving? Where are the bare spots? How's the soil? Mark plants you can't identify and give them time — many "weeds" turn out to be perennials.
  • Phase 3 — Soil: neglected yards usually mean compacted soil. Aerate, topdress with compost, and address drainage. On Winnipeg clay, this step makes or breaks the whole project.
  • Phase 4 — Rebuild: overseed thin areas (late Aug–mid Sept is ideal), edge beds, mulch, and prune overgrown shrubs. One or two cleaned beds per season is a realistic target.
  • Be realistic about timing. Winnipeg's short season means a full restoration is a multi-season project. Get the cleanup and soil right in year one; the pretty comes in year two.

Mulch beds done right: a homeowner's guideDepth, edging, and fabric myths: build a mulch bed that stays tidy all season.

Mulch is the cheapest upgrade a garden bed can get. It holds moisture in the soil, suppresses weeds, keeps roots cool in summer, and makes everything look finished. And most mulch jobs go wrong in the same handful of ways — all avoidable. Here is the whole job, done right, in one spring weekend.

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

Pick the right mulch. Shredded cedar or pine bark is the workhorse for Winnipeg beds: it knits together, stays put in wind, and breaks down slowly. Hardwood bark works too. Skip the dyed stuff — the colour fades by midsummer and hides thin coverage, so you end up using more of it. And skip fresh, uncomposted wood chips in planting beds: as they break down they pull nitrogen out of the soil, which starves your plants. Save the free arborist chips for paths, not beds.

Figure out how much you need. Measure the bed's length times width in feet, multiply by the depth you want in inches, then divide by 324. That gives you cubic yards. Example: a 10 × 4 ft bed at 3 inches deep is (10 × 4 × 3) ÷ 324 = 0.37 cubic yards — about 10 bags of the standard 2 cu ft size, which covers roughly 8 sq ft per bag at 3 inches. Buy 10% more than the math says. Running out mid-job is how beds end up with thin, weedy patches.

Prep the bed first. Mulch over weeds just hides them for a few weeks. Pull everything, roots and all, then cut a clean edge along the bed line with a spade or half-moon edger — that edge is what keeps grass from creeping in and mulch from spilling out. Water the soil well before mulching so you are locking moisture in, not drought.

Lay it 2–3 inches deep, no more. This is the number that matters. Under 2 inches and weeds push through; over 4 inches and you suffocate roots and invite rot. Rake it level. Then pull the mulch back 3–6 inches from every stem and trunk. The "mulch volcano" piled against a tree trunk is the most common mulching mistake in the city — it traps moisture against bark, and bark was never meant to stay wet. Trees die from it slowly, over years, and nobody connects the dots.

Do not put landscape fabric under it. Fabric sounds like a weed shortcut, but weeds root into the mulch layer above it anyway, and then you cannot pull them without ripping the fabric. Future you will hate past you. A clean edge and 3 inches of mulch beat fabric every time.

Time it for spring, after the soil warms. In Winnipeg that is usually May. Mulching too early, onto cold soil, just keeps the ground cold longer and slows your plants down. A light top-up in fall is fine if beds look thin going into winter, but the main job is spring.

The short version: Shredded bark, 2–3 inches, no volcanoes, no fabric, weed and edge first. One weekend, and your beds look after themselves all summer.

Small fence and gate fixes you can finish in an afternoonSagging gates, loose boards, and sticky latches — small fixes done in an afternoon.

Fences fail in predictable ways, and most of the common ones are an afternoon's work with basic tools. Here are the three repairs that cover the vast majority of sagging, rattling, and dragging.

Short on time? Skip to the short version ↓

The sagging gate. Gravity always wins eventually. A gate sags because the hinges loosen, the wood warps, or the latch-side corner just gets heavy over time. Start with the hinges: tighten every screw, and replace any that spin freely with longer exterior screws that bite into fresh wood in the post. If it still sags, add a diagonal brace — a board or an anti-sag turnbuckle kit running from the bottom hinge side up to the top latch side. That diagonal is what carries the weight; without it, every fix is temporary. For a heavy gate that will always sag, a small gate wheel on the latch side takes the load off entirely.

Loose or missing pickets. Pickets work loose from wind, frost, and kids. Re-fasten them with exterior screws rather than nails — screws hold through Winnipeg's freeze-thaw cycles where nails walk out. Drill a small pilot hole first so the picket does not split, especially near the ends. For a broken picket, pry it off, cut or buy a matching replacement, and prime or paint any cut ends before it goes up — raw end grain soaks up water and rots first.

The dragging or sticking gate. If the gate scrapes the ground or the latch no longer catches, something moved. Check whether the gate is still level and the posts are still plumb before "fixing" anything — in spring, frost heave shifts posts, and a heaved post often settles back on its own once the ground thaws and dries. Do not pour concrete around a post to fix a heave; you will just lock the problem in. If the gate drags permanently, trim the bottom with a saw. If the latch misses, adjust the latch hardware rather than forcing the gate — a few millimetres of adjustment beats a gate you have to lift to close.

A Winnipeg note on posts. Wood posts rot at the ground line, where wet meets dry — that is the weak point, not the middle of the post. When you set or reset a post, gravel at the base and good drainage matter more than concrete. And give spring-shifted fences a few weeks after thaw before deciding anything is broken; half of what looks like damage in April is just heave that has not settled.

Know when it is not a small fix. A post rotted through at ground level, a whole section leaning, or a gate frame that is twisted beyond what a brace can save — those are rebuilds, not repairs. There is no shame in that; fences have a lifespan. But do not spend three weekends patching a fence that needs replacing.

The short version: Sagging gate — longer screws, then a diagonal brace. Loose pickets — exterior screws, pilot holes, prime the cut ends. Dragging gate — check for heave first, adjust the latch, trim only if it is permanent. Afternoon jobs, every one of them.
