Storm day protocol: what to expect during a blizzard

Big storms raise the same questions every time. Here is how we handle them:

  • We watch the forecast, not the clock. When snowfall is forecast to hit your trigger, we plan the route in advance.
  • We clear within 12 hours of the snow stopping. During multi-day storms, we may do a mid-storm pass to keep driveways passable, then a final clear when it ends.
  • Route order is by trigger and location. Premium (2 cm) customers are cleared first, then Complete (4 cm), then Essential (6 cm) — grouped by street to keep things efficient.
  • Do not shovel ahead of us. If we are on the way, shoveling first just means we clear an empty driveway. Save your back.
  • Keep cars accessible. If possible, leave enough room for us to clear around your vehicles — we offer complimentary brushing for up to two cars per visit on request.
  • Windrow comes last. The city plow often makes its final pass after we have cleared. If your windrow appears after our visit, let us know and we will come back for it.

Severe blizzards with road closures may delay us — safety comes first. We will always communicate if the 12-hour window is at risk.

How to prepare for our visit

A few small things make each clearing visit faster and better:

  • Park with clearance in mind. Leave space around your vehicles so we can clear underneath and brush them off fully (complimentary on request).
  • Move bins and obstacles. Garbage bins, recycling, and anything left on the driveway should be pulled back before a storm.
  • Unlock gates. If we need side-gate access for a walkway, leave it unlocked on snow days.
  • Keep pets inside during clearing. Blowers are loud and throw snow — it is safer (and calmer) for everyone if pets stay in.
  • Flag anything new. Got a new garden bed, a delivery left on the walkway, or ice forming somewhere unusual? A quick text helps us adjust.
  • Do not salt right before we come. Fresh salt makes snow slushy and harder to clear cleanly. Salt after we have cleared for best results.

These are not requirements — we will clear regardless. They just help us do the best possible job.

❄️ Winter 8 guides

How to prepare your driveway for winter

Winnipeg averages about 126 cm of snow a year. A little prep in October saves you headaches in January:

  • Mark your edges. Put driveway markers or stakes along the edges before the ground freezes. It helps whoever clears your snow avoid chewing up grass or hitting hidden curbs.
  • Trim back branches. Anything hanging over the driveway will get heavy with snow and ice — cut it back now.
  • Move summer gear. Bikes, toys, and planters left on the driveway become obstacles buried under snow.
  • Check your drainage. Make sure downspouts direct water away from the driveway, or you'll get an ice rink by December.
  • Stock up on sand. Even with a clearing service, a bag of sand by the door helps on icy mornings between visits.

We appreciate yard markers — they keep lawns looking great in spring. Ask us about marking during your fall cleanup.

Snow clearing triggers explained: 2 cm vs 4 cm vs 6 cm

A "trigger" is the snowfall amount that sends the crew out. Here's how to pick:

  • 6 cm (Essential) — for people who don't mind a bit of snow and mainly want the big dumps handled. Most cost-effective.
  • 4 cm (Complete) — the sweet spot for most Transcona driveways. You will rarely wake up to an uncleared driveway.
  • 2 cm (Premium) — for anyone who needs reliable access every morning, or just hates seeing snow, period.

Winnipeg gets a lot of 2–4 cm dustings that add up. If you are on the fence, the 4 cm trigger covers the vast majority of winter mornings without the premium price.

What is windrow (and why should you care?)

Windrow is the ridge of hard, packed snow the city plow leaves across the end of your driveway. It is the heaviest, iciest snow you will deal with all winter — and it shows up after you have already cleared the rest.

Most budget services skip it. We include windrow clearing with Complete and Premium plans, and offer it as a $25/visit add-on for Essential. If you have ever tried to chip through a plow ridge with a shovel at 7 am, you know why it matters.

Ice dam prevention on your roof

Ice dams form when heat escaping into the attic melts roof snow unevenly. Meltwater refreezes at the colder eaves, backs up under shingles, and leaks into your home. Winnipeg's freeze-thaw cycles make this one of the most expensive winter roofing problems.

  • Insulate the attic to R-50. That's roughly 16–20 inches of blown-in insulation, evenly distributed. Seal air leaks around chimneys, vents, and light fixtures first.
  • Ventilate properly. Soffit vents at the eaves plus ridge vents at the peak create airflow that keeps the roof deck cold and melting even.
  • Roof-rake the bottom 4–6 feet after heavy snowfalls. Never chip at ice with tools — you'll damage shingles and gutters.
  • Watch for big icicles. Large icicles along the eaves mean water is refreezing there. Act before a full dam forms.
  • March–April is peak season. Daytime sun melts heavy snow, nights refreeze it. Keep valleys clear.

Clogged gutters make dams worse — another reason to get them cleaned in fall.

Pet-safe ice melt: what actually works

"Pet-friendly" is an unregulated marketing term. Many products with the label still contain chlorides that harm pets. Always read the ingredient list.

  • Truly safe means chloride-free. Look for modified urea or glycol-based products with zero sodium, calcium, magnesium, or potassium chloride.
  • Avoid: rock salt (cuts paws), calcium chloride (chemical burns), magnesium chloride (dangerous for dogs with kidney issues), and anything with ethylene glycol (antifreeze — extremely toxic).
  • Wipe paws after every walk regardless of product. Even "safer" melts dry and crack paw pads.
  • Shovel early so less melt product is needed in the first place.

Our $15 sanding applications (included in Premium) add traction with zero chemicals — the safest option for pet owners.

How to choose a snow clearing service

A bad snow contractor leaves you stranded on the worst mornings of the year. Here's what to ask before you sign:

  • What's the snowfall trigger? Make sure it's written into the contract — a vague "when it snows" gives you no recourse when they skip a 3 cm dump.
  • What's the response time? Get a service window in writing (e.g. "within 12 hours of snowfall ending"). Ask how they handle multi-day storms.
  • Exactly what's cleared? Driveway? Walkway? Steps? Windrow? Some companies skip the windrow — the heaviest, iciest part. Get the full list in writing.
  • Are they insured? Ask for a certificate of liability insurance and Workers' Compensation coverage. Keep it on file.
  • Get a written contract. Scope, trigger, pricing, payment terms, cancellation policy. Never rely on a handshake.

Red flags: no written contract, full upfront payment with no cancellation clause, no insurance, prices far below market.

What to do when your car is stuck in snow

Every Winnipeg driver will use this eventually. Safety first, then technique:

  • Clear the tailpipe FIRST. Before running the engine, make sure the exhaust is completely clear. A blocked tailpipe can fill the cabin with carbon monoxide in minutes.
  • Dig out the drive wheels. Clear snow from in front of, behind, and under the tires. If the car is resting on packed snow (high-centered), the tires can't grip no matter what you do.
  • Rock it gently. Shift between Drive and Reverse with light throttle, building momentum inches at a time. Stop fully before shifting. Momentum, not power, gets you out.
  • Turn off traction control. It cuts power when it detects slip — the opposite of what you want. Turn it back on once moving.
  • Add traction. Sand, kitty litter, or salt in the tire path. In a pinch: floor mats (rubber side down) or cardboard.
  • Know when to stop. 15–30 minutes of spinning with no progress? Stop before you damage the transmission. Call for help.

The best stuck-car story is the one that never happens — we offer complimentary snow brushing for parked vehicles on request, so you start every morning on pavement.

Frozen pipe prevention for Winnipeg winters

With frost reaching 7–8 feet deep and −30°C cold snaps, frozen pipes are a recurring Winnipeg hazard. Prevention beats repair:

  • Drain outdoor taps in fall. Shut off the interior valve, open the outside tap, disconnect hoses. Trapped water freezes back into the wall.
  • Insulate pipes in crawl spaces, garages, attics, and along exterior walls — especially north-facing ones.
  • Seal air leaks around outlets, vents, and pipe penetrations. Cold air on a pipe matters more than the thermostat.
  • Keep the heat on — never below 12°C, even when away. Open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls.
  • Drip on the coldest nights. A slow drip on the farthest faucet resists freezing during −35°C snaps.

If a pipe freezes: keep the faucet open, apply gentle heat (warm towels, hair dryer). Never use a blowtorch — the City of Winnipeg explicitly warns against it. Call 311 for city-side freezes.

🍂 Fall 5 guides

Fall cleanup checklist for Winnipeg homes

Do this before the first lasting snowfall and your spring self will thank you:

  • Clear all leaves. Matted leaves smother grass over winter and invite mold in spring.
  • Pick up branches and droppings. Anything left on the lawn gets pressed into the turf by snow.
  • Clean out garden beds. Remove dead annuals and cut back perennials.
  • Mark sprinkler heads and hidden edges. Flag anything a blower or shovel could hit.
  • Last mow short. Cut the grass a bit shorter than usual for the final mow — it reduces snow mold.

Why gutter cleaning matters before winter

Clogged gutters are a top contributor to ice dams, fascia rot, and foundation damage — all vastly more expensive than a cleaning.

  • Timing: late October to early November, after leaves fall but before the first lasting freeze.
  • Clogged gutters feed ice dams. Debris traps meltwater at the eaves where it refreezes and forces water under shingles.
  • Clear the downspouts too and make sure they discharge at least 4 feet from the foundation.
  • Weight kills gutters. Debris plus ice and snow load can pull gutters right off the fascia.

Clean gutters now, avoid a $2,000 roof repair in February. Ask about adding gutter cleaning to your fall cleanup.

Protecting young trees and shrubs for winter

Winnipeg's −30°C lows, drying winds, and freeze-thaw cycles are brutal on young plantings. A little fall prep goes a long way.

  • Water deeply before freeze-up. Soak the root zone in late October/early November. Winter drought kills more plants than cold alone.
  • Mulch after the ground freezes (late November): 4 inches of wood chips, kept 6 inches from the trunk. No "mulch volcanoes."
  • Wrap young trees with burlap from base to first branches to prevent sunscald and frost cracking. Remove in early April.
  • Guard against rodents with trunk guards 18–24 inches above expected snow depth.
  • Don't pile snow on plantings. It compacts, delays thaw, and can snap branches.

Fall cleanup is the natural time to install wraps, guards, and mulch — just ask.

Winterizing your lawn equipment

Stale fuel is the #1 cause of spring no-starts. An hour of fall maintenance saves a repair bill in April.

  • Fuel: stabilize or drain. Either fill ¾ with fresh gas plus stabilizer and run 5–10 minutes, or run the tank completely dry. Never store half-full and untreated.
  • Change the oil so fresh oil protects the engine all winter.
  • Clean the deck and filter. Scrape caked grass (disconnect the spark plug first), brush or replace the air filter.
  • Sharpen the blade now so the mower is truly ready in spring.
  • Batteries indoors on a smart maintainer, not bare concrete.
  • Don't forget the snowblower — fresh oil, check shear pins and belts before the first snowfall.
Leaf mulching vs bagging: what's better?

Mulch-mowing leaves into the lawn beats bagging in most cases — and the research backs it up:

  • Mulched leaves are free fertilizer. Roughly 1–2% nitrogen and up to 2% potassium — close to commercial fertilizer ratios. Can replace up to 25% of annual fertilizer needs.
  • It builds better soil. Shredded leaves add organic carbon, improving structure and moisture retention. Research found no harm to turf — and suppressed dandelion germination the next spring.
  • How: mow over dry leaves with a sharp blade, two passes in different directions. You should still see grass through the shredded material.
  • When to bag instead: if leaves are matted, wet, or so thick you can't see grass after mowing. Thick wet mats block light and trap moisture.
  • Shredded leaves don't cause thatch. Thatch comes from grass stems and roots, not leaves.

Our fall cleanup mulches leaves where appropriate and bags where it's not — ask about it when you book.

🌱 Spring & Summer 10 guides

When to aerate your lawn in Manitoba

The best time to aerate in Winnipeg is early fall (September) or spring (May) — when the grass is actively growing but the heat is not stressing it.

Signs your lawn needs it: water pools instead of soaking in, the soil feels rock-hard, or thin/bare patches keep coming back despite watering and fertilizing. Clay-heavy Manitoba soil compacts easily, so most lawns benefit from aeration every 1–2 years.

Snow mold: what it is and how to beat it

Those circular straw-coloured patches that appear when snow melts? That's snow mold — a fungal disease. Gray mold affects blades only and usually recovers; pink mold can kill crowns and need reseeding.

  • Prevention starts in fall: final mow at 2–3 inches, clear all leaves, stop nitrogen fertilizer 6 weeks before frost, keep thatch under ½ inch.
  • Don't pile snow in one spot — concentrated piles keep soil cold and wet longer, creating ideal mold conditions.
  • Spring treatment: gently rake matted areas on a dry day to improve airflow. Most lawns recover naturally as temperatures pass 10–15°C.
  • Overseed bare patches where pink mold killed the crowns.

Check shaded areas and low spots first — anywhere snow lingered longest.

Spring dethatching for Manitoba lawns

Thatch is the spongy brown layer between grass blades and soil. Over ½ inch, it blocks water, harbors disease, and starves roots.

  • Timing: mid to late April, once the lawn is dry and daytime highs are above 10°C for a week. Never work wet clay — it compacts.
  • Test first: cut a small wedge and measure. Under ½ inch? Skip it this year.
  • How: rent a power rake, set tines to just tickle the soil, two passes at right angles. It looks brutal; that's normal.
  • Clean up thoroughly — leaving pulled thatch defeats the purpose.
  • Follow with aeration and overseeding for the full lawn revival.
Overseeding: timing and best seed for Winnipeg

Overseeding thickens thin lawns and repairs winter damage. Timing determines whether seed becomes grass or bird food.

  • Best window: late August to mid-September. Warm soil, cool air, fall rains, minimal weed competition.
  • Best seed: Kentucky bluegrass blends (3+ varieties for disease resistance). Handles Winnipeg extremes and self-repairs.
  • Rate: 2–5 lbs per 1,000 sq ft. Buy quality seed — cheap bags contain weed seeds.
  • Prep matters more than seed: mow short, rake to expose soil, aerate first. Water lightly 2–3 times daily until germination.

Aeration + overseeding is our classic fall package — ask for a free quote.

Summer watering schedule for Winnipeg lawns

Deep, infrequent watering builds deep roots. Daily sprinkling builds shallow, drought-prone turf.

  • The 1-inch rule: lawns need ~1 inch per week including rainfall. Water deeply 1–2 times per week, not daily.
  • Water 4–10 AM. Less evaporation, foliage dries before nightfall. Never midday, never late evening.
  • The footprint test: if footprints stay visible, it's time to water.
  • Mow high: 2.5–3 inches. Taller grass shades roots and cuts water loss.
  • Let it go dormant in heat waves. Brown Kentucky bluegrass in extreme heat is surviving, not dying. It greens up when temperatures drop.

Check the City of Winnipeg's current water conservation rules before setting a schedule — restrictions vary year to year.

Watering through the seasons: a Winnipeg calendar

Your lawn's water needs change dramatically across Winnipeg's seasons. Here's the year-round playbook:

  • Spring (April–May): hold off. Snowmelt usually provides plenty of moisture. Don't start watering until the soil is dry 2 inches down and daytime highs are consistently above 10°C. Watering too early on saturated clay just pools and compacts.
  • Late spring (May–June): wake it up. As growth kicks in, shift to deep watering 1–2 times per week. This is prime time for new seed — keep seeded areas consistently moist with light daily watering until germination.
  • Summer (July–August): deep and early. 1 inch per week including rain, between 4–10 AM. Mow high (2.5–3 inches) to shade roots. Let bluegrass go dormant in extreme heat rather than fighting it.
  • Early fall (September): taper down. Cooler nights and fall rains reduce needs. Keep watering new fall seed, but established lawns need less. This is the time to aerate — it helps fall rain soak in instead of running off clay.
  • Late fall (October–November): the deep soak. Before the ground freezes, give lawns, trees, and shrubs one last deep watering. Winter drought — roots unable to draw frozen water while wind strips moisture — kills more plants than cold alone. This is the most overlooked watering of the year.
  • Winter: hands off outdoors. No watering needed, but make sure downspouts direct meltwater away from foundations during thaws.

If you have in-ground irrigation, get it blown out before the first hard freeze — frozen lines crack and the repair bill is ugly.

Dealing with Winnipeg's heavy clay soil

Winnipeg sits on dense Red River clay — slow to drain, quick to compact, rock-hard when dry. It's behind half of local lawn problems.

  • Compost is the #1 fix. Spread 2–4 inches and work into the top 6–8 inches. This permanently improves structure — repeat annually.
  • Aerate 1–2 times per year. Core aeration physically opens clay. The single highest-ROI service for Winnipeg lawns.
  • Gypsum helps as a supplement (~40 lbs per 1,000 sq ft), not a replacement for compost.
  • Don't add small amounts of sand — it makes clay worse. Only helps at 50%+ ratios with lots of organic matter.
  • Never work wet clay. Wait until it's moist but crumbly or you'll create brick-like clods.

Aeration is our flagship clay-soil service — and the reason we recommend it so often in Winnipeg.

Spring fertilizer schedule for Manitoba lawns

Timing matters more than product. Fertilizing too early is one of the most common Manitoba lawn mistakes:

  • Wait until grass is growing. Don't fertilize until soil hits 10°C and the lawn is greening — typically mid-to-late May in Winnipeg. Cold soil wastes product.
  • The Manitoba schedule: late spring (mid-May–June, high-nitrogen) → early summer (late June, balanced, light rate) → late summer (August, slow-release) → fall winterizer (late Sept–Oct, high-potassium). The fall application matters most.
  • Read the bag: N-P-K. Nitrogen = leaf growth, phosphorus = roots, potassium = stress resistance. Spring wants nitrogen; fall wants potassium.
  • Aerate first on clay. Nutrients can't reach roots through compacted clay — aerate so fertilizer soaks in instead of running off.
  • Apply on dry grass, then water in. Morning is ideal. Never apply extra "for good measure" — overdoing nitrogen burns lawns.
Lawn grubs: how to spot and treat them

White grubs (beetle larvae) eat grass roots — but the animals digging for them often do worse damage than the grubs themselves.

  • Signs: brown patches that expand, spongy turf, grass that lifts like a carpet. The giveaway: skunks, raccoons, or crows tearing up the lawn.
  • Confirm before treating. Cut a 30×30 cm sod square, fold it back, count. Around 8–10 grubs per square foot warrants action.
  • Timing: young grubs near the surface in August–September are the easiest target. Match treatment to the vulnerable window.
  • Treatment: beneficial nematodes (applied to moist soil in the evening, pet/kid safe) are the eco-friendly standard. Btg products target grubs with low non-target risk.
  • Prevention: mow high (2.5–3 inches), aerate, dethatch. A vigorous lawn outgrows moderate grub feeding.

Healthy, aerated lawns resist grubs naturally — another reason we push aeration so hard in Winnipeg.

Restoring a neglected or overgrown yard

Inherited a jungle? A yard that's been ignored for a season or more can come back — but work in stages, not all at once:

  • Phase 1 — Clear: remove trash, debris, and dead material. Cut overgrown grass in stages — never more than one-third of the blade at once. Start high, work down over several mowings.
  • Phase 2 — Assess: once you can see the ground, evaluate. What's worth saving? Where are the bare spots? How's the soil? Mark plants you can't identify and give them time — many "weeds" turn out to be perennials.
  • Phase 3 — Soil: neglected yards usually mean compacted soil. Aerate, topdress with compost, and address drainage. On Winnipeg clay, this step makes or breaks the whole project.
  • Phase 4 — Rebuild: overseed thin areas (late Aug–mid Sept is ideal), edge beds, mulch, and prune overgrown shrubs. One or two cleaned beds per season is a realistic target.
  • Be realistic about timing. Winnipeg's short season means a full restoration is a multi-season project. Get the cleanup and soil right in year one; the pretty comes in year two.

Print this out and work through it before the first lasting snowfall:

  • Rake and remove all leaves — matted leaves smother grass and invite mold.
  • Pick up branches, twigs, and droppings from the lawn and beds.

Print this out and work through it once the snow melts:

  • Rake up winter debris — branches, dead leaves, and leftover sand.
  • Check for snow mold — rake matted patches lightly to let air in.
  • Edge garden beds for a clean look and to keep grass out.
  • Test soil and top-dress thin spots with compost or topsoil.
  • Overseed bare patches once daytime temps stay above 10°C.
  • Apply spring fertilizer on the schedule for your grass type.
  • Inspect for grubs — lift a square of turf; more than 5 grubs per sq ft needs action.
  • Reconnect hoses and check outdoor taps for freeze damage.
  • Sharpen mower blades and set cutting height to 3 inches.
🖨️ Printable: Summer lawn care checklist

Print this out and keep it on the fridge for the mowing season:

  • Mow at 3 inches — taller grass shades roots and crowds out weeds.
  • Water deeply, 1 inch per week — early morning is best; check city restrictions.
  • Leave clippings — they return nitrogen to the soil (unless the lawn is diseased).
  • Weed weekly — pull them before they seed; 10 minutes a week beats a weekend marathon.
  • Watch for brown patches — could be grubs, drought, or fungus; investigate before treating.
  • Trim hedges after the spring growth flush hardens off (late June).
  • Mulch garden beds 2–3 inches deep to hold moisture and suppress weeds.
  • Clean mower deck monthly — caked grass reduces cut quality.
